Notes and open questions

Debated points (the origins of Yiddish, several etymologies, speaker numbers) are labelled as debated in the video. Numbers of speakers are estimates. These items could not be fully verified and were softened or flagged in the narration:

Fact-check notes (45)
  • Chapter 1: Scholars largely accept that bentshn (to bless) and leyenen (to read) come from Romance languages, but some details are still debated. The video follows the YIVO account.
  • Chapter 2: Sources give either 1028 or 1040 as the year Rabbeynu Gershom died. The video uses 1028.
  • Chapter 2: The names Bunem (from bon homme) and Shneyer (from senior, or from Hebrew 'two lights') have uncertain origins, and the video labels them as such.
  • Chapter 3: Scholars debate whether the Worms Mahzor was made in Worms or in Würzburg, so the narration does not commit to either.
  • Chapter 3: The Shmuel-bukh and Melokhim-bukh are often dated to the fifteenth century. The video gives the broader 1300s–1400s range found in its sources.
  • Chapter 4: The Council of Four Lands is conventionally dated to around 1580, though some historians place its beginnings earlier.
  • Chapter 4: The share of Ashkenazi Jews who spoke Yiddish around 1930 is estimated, not counted. 'Nearly all' fits the figure of about 13 million speakers but may be generous.
  • Chapter 6: The exact year the Soviet spelling reform dropped the final letter forms is uncertain, so the video says the 1920s–30s.
  • Chapter 6: Hasidic pronunciation differs between communities, especially between Polish- and Hungarian-rooted groups. Throughout the series, the respellings follow one common pattern rather than every local variant.
  • Chapter 7: The abbreviation ב״ה is traditionally read in two ways, 'blessed be God' and 'with God's help'. The video gives the first.
  • Chapter 9: Researchers debate how far case and gender have weakened in Hasidic Yiddish. One London study reports their complete loss, and the video presents the question as an open debate.
  • Chapter 11: Both 'ton a trakht' and 'gebn a trakht' are attested, and sources disagree on which is more common.
  • Chapter 12: The claim that most Hasidim say nisht rather than nit is a generalization. Usage varies by community.
  • Chapter 13: Many Hasidic speakers use benkl for 'chair', but in standard Yiddish benkl means a small bench and the usual word for chair is shtul.
  • Chapter 14: Standard Yiddish for carrots is mern. The Hasidic form mayern used in the video still needs checking against dictionaries.
  • Chapter 15: The video says some Yiddish dialects pronounce final consonants voiceless, but the evidence for this is limited.
  • Chapter 16: Estimates of how much of everyday Yiddish text comes from Hebrew and Aramaic vary by source. The video presents its 12–20% figure as an estimate.
  • Chapter 16: The word take (really) is often traced to Slavic, but its origin is not settled.
  • Chapter 17: Many scholars credit Slavic influence for the single reflexive zikh and for verb prefixes that mark aspect, but not all agree. The video says 'many scholars'.
  • Chapter 18: The video describes today's Hungarian Hasidic speech as 'Central type, with Hungarian coloring'. This is a simplification.
  • Chapter 19: Not every Brooklyn community speaks the same Yiddish. Lubavitch Crown Heights uses a more Lithuanian-type Yiddish than the Hasidic Central type taught here.
  • Chapter 20: Sources give 1976 or 1977 for the incorporation of Kiryas Joel. The video uses 1976 and says 'soon after'.
  • Chapter 20: Sources differ on whether the Satmar Rebbe arrived in New York in 1946 or 1947. The video uses 1946.
  • Chapter 21: The Daf Yomi was proposed in Vienna in 1923, before Reb Meir Shapiro led his yeshiva in Lublin. The video still calls him the Lubliner, as he is commonly known.
  • Chapter 21: The word grager (Purim noisemaker) is traced to Polish in the YIVO Encyclopedia, while other accounts link it to a Germanic word for rattling.
  • Chapter 22: Perl's best-known satire was printed in Hebrew, and its Yiddish version was found only in 1937. Saying he 'used Yiddish anyway' is therefore only loosely accurate.
  • Chapter 22: Sources date the loss of Peretz's license to 1887 or 1888. Ettinger's works were mostly printed long after he wrote them.
  • Chapter 23: Sources date Abraham Cahan's return to the Forverts to 1902 or 1903. The video uses 1902.
  • Chapter 23: Readers disagree over whether Hersh Raseyner was the narrator's former teacher or a fellow student. The video follows YIVO, which says teacher.
  • Chapter 25: Sources disagree on several dates, including Naftule Brandwein's arrival (1908 or 1909), Dave Tarras's birth year (1895 to 1898), and the song 'Es brent' (1936 or 1938). The video gives common dates, hedged where needed.
  • Chapter 26: The expression 'ikh darf es vi a lokh in kop' is used in Yiddish, but it may have started in American Jewish English.
  • Chapter 27: Sources give 1917 or 1918 for the founding of the Khoyrev school network, so the video says 'late 1910s'.
  • Chapter 27: Estimates of the Forverts' peak circulation in the 1920s range from about 250,000 to 275,000. The video says 'around a quarter of a million'.
  • Chapter 27: The Yiddish of Williamsburg and Kiryas Joel is widely thought to come mainly from Hungarian Yiddish, though Galician and Polish varieties also contributed.
  • Chapter 28: Counts of David Boder's survivor interviews range from about 109 to 130. The video says scholars count around 130.
  • Chapter 28: That Stalin ordered the killing of Solomon Mikhoels is presented as the view of many historians, not as established fact.
  • Chapter 29: The suggestion that US surveys undercount Yiddish speakers is a reasonable inference, not a documented finding.
  • Chapter 30: The word dafke is glossed as 'necessarily', following Katz. Today it more often means 'precisely', 'deliberately' or 'out of spite'.
  • Chapter 30: Modern Eastern Yiddish has lost the simple past tense, but Old Yiddish and some Western dialects kept it.
  • Chapter 31: Estimates of Jewish immigration to the US between 1880 and 1924 range from about 2 to 2.5 million. The video says 'roughly two million'.
  • Chapter 32: The figure that about three quarters of speakers used Southern dialects is an approximation from a single general source.
  • Chapter 32: The date and setting of the lecture where Max Weinreich heard 'a language is a dialect with an army and navy' are uncertain. The video says only that he made the line famous in 1945.
  • Chapter 34: Standard Yiddish accepts both zaynen and zenen ('are'), even though the video implies zenen is only Hasidic.
  • Chapter 35: Sources disagree on which edition of Harkavy's dictionary appeared in 1928, so the video calls it 'an enlarged edition'.
  • Chapter 36: Digitizing the Spielberg Digital Yiddish Library began in 1997, but free online access came later, around 2009. The video gives 1997 as the start of the project.

What is real and what is fictional

The prince, his kingdom, his palaces, his household and the scenes of his daily life are fictional. Everything said about real history, real palaces, titles, protocol, households, objects, brands and places was checked against reference sources while the film was made (October 2026). Royal authority, wealth and privileges differ greatly between countries and institutions; nothing in the film claims real-world authority for the fictional prince. Opinions are labelled as opinions in the narration.

Notes and open questions

  • Monarchies: Sources disagree on whether some monarchies, such as Liechtenstein, Monaco, Morocco and Jordan, count as constitutional or absolute, and any link between a monarchy's type and its wealth is offered as a possibility, not a proven finding (Chapter 1).
  • Monarchies: The trend toward 'quiet luxury' is something commentators have noticed rather than measured, and Aristotle's examples of generous public spending are summarised rather than listed one by one (Chapter 1).
  • Succession: Liechtenstein's reported 2026 move to let the eldest child inherit regardless of sex could not be confirmed, and Saudi succession, long passed between brothers, has recently shifted to a younger generation (Chapter 21).
  • Succession: Dating constitutional monarchy in Britain to 1689 reflects a common historical view rather than a single legal event, and the claim that aristocrats own more than a third of British land is one estimate among several (Chapter 21).
  • Titles: Heirs' titles are given in their traditional forms, the first recorded use of 'Royal Highness' in 1633 and the description of earl as 'among the oldest' ranks rest on limited sources, and the count of 142 Spanish grandee marquesses may change over time (Chapters 2 and 18).
  • Recent events: The accession date of Norway's King Haakon VIII and the Royal Collection's online total of 300,093 items each come from a single source, while Kuwait's 2024 suspension of its parliament is stated from widely available information (Chapters 16 and 31).
  • Royal households: Staff numbers, the number of charities the British royal family supports, tour planning times and the use of confidentiality agreements are given loosely or as reported, because published figures vary (Chapters 2 and 8).
  • Royal households: Descriptions of how the Danish, Dutch, Swedish and British royal households are organised, and of how invitations are assessed, are kept general because detailed sources were limited (Chapter 30).
  • Money: The £72 million figure covers a single 2023 state occasion, not all ceremonial costs, and the share of Dartmoor owned by the Duchy of Cornwall is not given (Chapters 1 and 32).
  • Money: The Duke of Cornwall's income tax of about £7.8 million rests on press reports, and the value of a Golden Fleece collar, about €50,000, is a published estimate rather than an official figure (Chapters 19 and 30).
  • Watches and jewels: Reported prices for the most expensive watches differ between sources, the oldest watchmaker title is a widely held description, the reason for the emerald cut is only 'often said', and the Koh-i-Noor's early history is still debated (Chapter 7).
  • The Peacock Throne: Its cost of twice the Taj Mahal is a traditional estimate, its replacement was lost only around the time of the 1857 rebellion, and the idea that narwhal tusk was prized above gold is a repeated saying (Chapter 5).
  • Furniture and textiles: Dates for the club chair, the wing chair and the Jacquard loom are approximate, and the 1952 date of a bed maker's royal warrant is the company's own claim (Chapters 4, 5 and 26).
  • Palaces: Room counts use different counting methods and cannot be compared closely, Madrid's 'largest palace in Europe' status depends on floor area, and the Forbidden City's workforce of over a million is a traditional estimate (Chapter 3).
  • Palaces: Figures for the Hall of Mirrors, dates for several palaces and royal workshops, credit for Buckingham Palace's Grand Staircase, the dating of the Alhambra, and the survival of the Würzburg staircase in 1945 are given broadly or as widely reported (Chapter 29).
  • Life at Versailles: The king's waking time of about 8:30, the meaning of moving his bedchamber, courtiers bowing to the empty bed, and when English bedchamber posts ended or changed names are approximate or based on period accounts (Chapter 4).
  • Dining: The modern state banquet is credited to Louis-Philippe of France, but no specific date or guest number could be verified, and the 1682 to 1789 span for public royal dining at Versailles is approximate (Chapters 25 and 27).
  • Dining: The military comparison for kitchen teams, staffing for Russian-style service, seating plans and serving from left or right are described as typical practice, which varies between countries and households (Chapters 9 and 27).
  • Household customs: Ironing newspapers, measuring place settings with sticks, the 'several hundred' palace clocks, the length of the morning bagpipe performance and pages as trainee footmen are all presented as reported rather than confirmed (Chapters 8 and 26).
  • Clothing: Old laws on who could wear purple silk or cloth of gold varied and faded only gradually, the costs of court coats are not given, and formal dress accessories and court dress rules are customary and differ by country (Chapter 6).
  • Ceremonial dress: How often the robe of state is worn, when army uniforms became practical, the order of medals, and the meaning of crown arches and the orb are given broadly or as British practice only (Chapter 17).
  • Regalia: The Crown Jewels gem count is rounded, visitor numbers and throne and crown weights are as reported, and which Japanese regalia items were physically present in 2019 is not known (Chapters 17 and 22).
  • Honours: The Order of the Garter's 1348 founding date is traditional, the Order of the Elephant's early origins and its badge count are approximate, and crediting Samuel Pepys with odd-numbered gun salutes and an Arabic origin for 'fanfare' are both uncertain (Chapters 19 and 33).
  • Ceremonies: The seven-hundred-year age of the Ceremony of the Keys is traditional, the weekday of the monarch's meetings with the Prime Minister is not given, and the privacy of those meetings and the carriage tradition for new ambassadors are described as custom (Chapters 20 and 27).
  • Ceremonies: Details of anthems, guards of honour, speeches, state visits, arrival arrangements and security are described in general terms because practice varies by country (Chapters 12, 13, 20 and 32).
  • Gardens: Dates for Pasargadae, Tivoli and the Machine de Marly are approximate, the Lahore canal and Cyrus the Younger's remark come from reports, and the Capability Brown park count and the origin of the word 'ha-ha' are uncertain (Chapter 10).
  • Fountains: Stories of fountain keepers starting jets in sequence or signalling by whistle, and of guests arriving at Peterhof by boat, are traditions, while the distance to Peterhof's springs and its designer's links are approximate (Chapters 3, 10, 23 and 27).
  • Royal transport: Whether the monarch's personal car mascot is still used is unconfirmed, the Duke of Edinburgh's remark about his car is only reported, and the extent of the old royal stables is described generally (Chapter 11).
  • Yachts and trains: Royal yachts in ancient Egypt depend on how the term is defined, cruise timings and train makeup are given loosely, cabin comfort claims come from a manufacturer, and the superyacht fleet share is one agency's figure (Chapters 12 and 28).
  • Resorts and hotels: The outcome of Badrutt's 1864 wager, St. Moritz's sunshine claim and the Savoy's electric firsts are presented as 'said' or 'generally credited', and only the year is given for the Emirates Palace opening (Chapters 13, 24 and 28).
  • Spas and baths: The Harrogate spring's discovery date is given as the late sixteenth century, the Latin meaning of 'spa' is probably a modern invention, and early Islamic bathhouses are described as suggestive rather than conclusive (Chapter 14).
  • Music, games and libraries: Haydn's famous remark is second-hand, the crowd of over twelve thousand at Vauxhall in 1749 is a period claim, and several library and court details come from widely available rather than checked sources (Chapters 15, 16 and 22).
  • Modern buildings: 'Form follows function' shortens the original wording, the first steel-framed skyscraper is debated, and the smart-device security figure comes from a single research group (Chapter 24).
  • Retreats and travel: Medieval travelling courts, Ludwig's brief stay at Neuschwanstein and the dates for Liechtenstein's formation are described broadly, and some hotel, theatre and word-origin details come from widely available sources (Chapter 23).
  • Invented details: The prince is fictional, and the colour-coding of papers in his official boxes is made up for the film (Chapter 26).
  • No free recording of footsteps on marble was found, so palace scenes use fountain and indoor ambience instead.
  • Ambient sound is used only at the opening and close of chapters, from the few free recordings available (fountain, birdsong, sea, rain, clock, distant fireworks, a door); most of each chapter has narration over music only.
  • No new pictures were generated for this film; it reuses the existing prince stills and motion clips, free Wikimedia Commons photographs of real places and objects, and drawn title, timeline and table cards. So some subjects are shown with a nearby related photo rather than the exact object.
  • Some prince stills and clips appear in more than one chapter.
  • Narration is a synthetic English voice; some foreign names may be pronounced imperfectly.
  • Music is public-domain or Creative Commons classical recordings from Wikimedia Commons, chosen to be instrumental.
